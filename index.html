How much does a long USB cable cost you?

Every charging cable is a pair of thin copper wires with some resistance. Push 3 amps through them and part of the energy turns into heat in the cable instead of charge in the battery. The calculator below shows how much.

How the numbers are worked out

Copper resistance per metre comes from the American wire gauge table. Current flows down one wire and back up the ground wire, so the copper path is twice the cable length. The voltage lost is current × resistance (Ohm's law), and the power lost is current² × resistance. The calculator ignores connector contact resistance, which in a worn plug can be as large as the wire itself.

What the results mean in practice

At 5 V, a half-volt drop matters: the phone sees 4.5 V and may step its current down to stay safe, so charging gets slower. This is why USB power delivery raises the voltage instead of the current for fast charging — at 20 V the same cable loses the same volts, but they are a much smaller share of the total.

A long cable is convenient at a bedside or in a car's back seat, and a 1.8 m braided lead such as the anker a8050 is a reasonable trade-off at phone currents of 2–3 A. At laptop currents, short and thick wins. The USB-IF Power Delivery page lists which cables are rated for 3 A and 5 A.

Rule of thumb: if a cable gets noticeably warm while charging, it is wasting enough power to slow the charge.
